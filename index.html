Recovering activity from the brain’s folds

Current project. UCSF, since July 2026.

Biophysical forward modeling and Bayesian reconstruction of sulcal sources for ECoG decoding

Question
ECoG arrays rest on the crowns of cortical folds, but much of the cortex involved in speech and movement lies deeper, in the superior temporal sulcus, central sulcus and Sylvian fissure. Can a physical model of the anatomy recover that activity well enough to improve decoding?
My role
I’m developing the source-localization framework. I build boundary-element forward models from FreeSurfer anatomy with OpenMEEG, and implement sparse Bayesian learning and adaptive beamforming in Python.
Why it matters
Recovering sulcal activity could let the same surface recordings carry more information for decoding.
Status
In progress. Better decoding is the goal, not yet a result. I’m benchmarking the methods with simulations and held-out electrodes.

Where this leads: reading more from the electrodes we already have, and, through the same head models, writing back to the brain.

Evaluation, groundwork, supervisors and tools

Evaluation

Controlled simulations, cross-validation and held-out-electrode prediction, measuring source-localization accuracy, signal reconstruction, and robustness to noise and to errors in the head model.

Groundwork

In 2025, at a neurotechnology startup, I built a modular ECoG source-modeling prototype (MRI segmentation, CT/MRI registration, finite-element meshing, cortical source spaces and standard coordinate transforms). I generated synthetic recordings with gamma bursts, event-related synchronization and desynchronization, traveling waves and nuisance sources, and trained STTU-Net, a convolutional–Transformer model with spectral and graph regularization, to reconstruct cortical signals. Interactive 3D diagnostics check electrode–anatomy alignment and forward and inverse outputs.

Supervisors

Srikantan Nagarajan and Edward F. Chang

Tools

FreeSurfer, OpenMEEG and Python

Decoding attempted speech from ECoG

Collaboration ongoing. Began during my UCSF visiting appointment, 2025–26.

Advancing neuroprosthetic speech decoding from ECoG recordings

Question
For a clinical-trial participant with severe paralysis, how far can an ECoG-to-text decoder for attempted silent speech improve through algorithms alone, without new neural recordings?
My role
I co-developed the framework. I tested spectral features up to 300 Hz against control features, built multi-GPU training pipelines for the neural decoders, and extended language-model refinement of candidate transcripts.
Why it matters
Accuracy and speed decide whether a speech neuroprosthesis can support everyday conversation.
Status
I contributed to the team’s offline result of 7.8% mean word error rate at an average of 83 words per minute, with a vocabulary of over 1,000 words. I’m co-first author of the abstract accepted for presentation at Neuroscience 2026.

Where this leads: decoders for speech and movement in everyday use, and decoders that catch their own mistakes.

Features, decoders, language models and collaborators

Features

STFT-derived spectral features up to 300 Hz alongside low-frequency ECoG. Matched-seed experiments, frequency-band ablations, and residual, shuffled and duplicated-feature controls tested for information beyond signal redundancy and larger input dimensionality.

Decoders

Memory-efficient multi-GPU PyTorch training for CNN–GRU decoders with connectionist temporal classification (CTC), plus Conformer ensembles. Automated training, beam-search and n-gram language-model experiments.

Language-model refinement

Extended LLM-based candidate refinement with QLoRA fine-tuning and phoneme-informed prompts. Benchmarked five LLMs and candidate-merging strategies using block-grouped cross-validation and validation-based merger selection.

People

Supervisors: David Moses and Edward F. Chang. Co-first author: Ekaterina Morozova. Co-author: Ankit Kumar.

Mapping brain activity to natural behavior

Current project. UCSF, since July 2026.

Multimodal brain–behavior mapping for naturalistic neural decoding

Question
Does neural activity during spontaneous, unscripted behavior carry information beyond basic movement kinematics?
My role
I’m building a framework that pairs intracranial EEG (iEEG) with video, audio and language, measures behavior automatically with pose estimation and vision–language models, and decodes movement and video representations from neural activity.
Why it matters
Clinical recordings capture hours of natural behavior. Measuring it automatically makes them searchable by behavior and usable for decoding.
Status
In progress. Evaluations use held-out recording days, movement baselines and temporal-mismatch controls.

Where this leads: decoders that follow natural behavior across recording days.

Measurement, pipelines, evaluation and supervisors

Behavior measurement

Pose estimation and vision–language models, plus pretrained video representations (V-JEPA), build paired neural–behavioral datasets without additional manual annotation.

Pipelines

Python pipelines for high-gamma feature extraction, continuous movement decoding and prediction of video representations from neural activity, with reproducible data provenance and local processing of sensitive clinical data.

Evaluation

Held-out recording days, movement baselines and temporal-mismatch controls test whether decoding generalizes and whether neural activity captures behavior beyond basic movement kinematics.

Supervisors

Srikantan Nagarajan and Edward F. Chang

Reading the brain, and one day writing to it

My interest is brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) as a whole, for speech and for movement. An interface is a loop: it records what the brain intends, decodes it into words or motion, and returns the result to the person. The four directions below go once around that loop, from reading the brain to the long-term goal of writing to it.

Across all four, I work in the same three ways: modeling where a signal comes from, testing decoders with controls that show what they really use, and designing for the loop that closes through the person.

  1. Read · Near term · brain →to electrodes

    How much more can the electrodes we already have tell us?

    Surface arrays rest on the crowns of cortical folds, but much of the cortex for speech and movement lies inside the folds: the superior temporal sulcus and Sylvian fissure for speech, the central sulcus for the hand. I want to find out how much of that hidden activity can be recovered with models of each person’s anatomy, and whether that changes what decoders can do.

    Questions about reading
    • Does activity recovered from inside the folds carry information a decoder can use, beyond what the surface contacts already record?
    • Which frequency bands carry speech and movement information, once controls rule out the gain from simply adding inputs?

    Built on: source reconstruction for ECoG (current project), the 2025 source-modeling prototype and frequency-band controls in speech decoding.

  2. Decode · Near term · electrodes →to decoder →to output

    Can decoders follow speech and movement as people actually use them?

    Speech and finger decoding have mostly used cued tasks. I want decoders for speech and movement that follow unscripted behavior, keep working from one day to the next, and use what the two share: both are laid out along the same strip of sensorimotor cortex.

    Questions about decoding
    • Do decoders learned from hours of natural behavior transfer to the cued tasks that speech and motor BCIs are trained on, and back?
    • What would continuous control of individual fingers need from cortical recordings that it did not need from muscle signals?

    Built on: speech decoding from ECoG (SfN 2026), brain–behavior mapping (current project), BETA (macaque M1 and PMd, 2024) and finger decoding from high-density surface EMG (2023–25).

  3. Close the loop · Next · output →to eyes and ears →to brain

    Can the person and the decoder correct each other?

    Once an interface is in use, the loop closes through the person: they see or hear the result and adjust, and the brain responds when an output is wrong. I built an ECoG detector for those error responses, and iCrutch, a reading interface that predicts involuntary eye movements and shifts the text to offset them. I want decoders that use such signals while they run.

    Questions about closing the loop
    • Can error responses recorded from the brain correct a speech or movement decoder as it runs?
    • How should a decoder adapt during use, and how quickly must it respond for the person to stay in control?

    Built on: the ECoG error-potential detector (2025) and iCrutch (2026).

  4. Write · Long term · sensors →to encoder →to stimulation →to brain

    Can an interface put information back into the brain?

    A hand moved by a decoder still cannot feel what it holds. Stimulating sensory cortex can evoke touch felt in the hand, and stimulating visual cortex can evoke points of light, so an interface could send what its sensors detect back into the brain. This is a long-term goal, not current work. By reciprocity, the head model that says what a contact records also predicts where its stimulation current goes. How neurons respond to that field is the harder part, and the part I want to learn.

    Questions about writing
    • How should what a prosthetic hand senses be encoded as stimulation, so that it is felt as touch and helps with grasping?
    • Can one device record and stimulate in the same loop, without the stimulation corrupting what the decoder reads?
    • Can models of individual anatomy aim stimulation at cortex inside a sulcus?

    Built on: the forward models in my source-reconstruction project and behavioral testing in a prosthetic-vision rehabilitation study (PLoVR, 2023–24).

From engineering to human neurophysiology

I came to neuroscience through engineering. At Johns Hopkins I double-majored in biomedical engineering and computer science, with a minor in robotics. My early projects ranged widely: VR task code for a prosthetic-vision rehabilitation study, a patient-securement device for interventional radiology, EMG decoding for prosthetic hands and, during a summer in Beijing, intracortical decoding in macaques.

Since 2025 I have worked with human ECoG, first at a neurotechnology startup and then in Edward Chang’s lab at UCSF, where I am now a clinical research coordinator. Across these projects I have built complete pipelines, from anatomy and raw signals to trained models, and designed the controls that show what a decoder is really using.

  2. Feb – May 2026

    Independent course project

    Computer Integrated Surgery Lab, Johns Hopkins University. Supervisors: Kemar Green and Peter Kazanzides.

    iCrutch, a closed-loop reading interface for nystagmus: on a held-out hybrid test set it reduced median simulated retinal-slip speed by 92%. Accepted for an oral presentation at the Bárány Society Meeting, 2026.

    More on iCrutch

    The interface shifts on-screen text to offset involuntary eye movements in nystagmus. I built a subject-disjoint hybrid dataset (real GazeBaseVR reading trajectories plus simulated pendular and jerk nystagmus) and a joint GRU classifier–forecaster that predicts 200 ms of displacement from 120 Hz gaze, with uncertainty-weighted fusion, classifier gating, smoothing and motion constraints.

    Besides the 92% reduction, it kept 56.7% of pathology windows below the 3°/s target and reached 0.106° displacement error over 200 ms. A separate webcam proof of concept (MediaPipe, about 30 Hz) adds calibration and blink and artifact gating.

  4. May – Aug 2025

    A neurotechnology startup.

    An ECoG detector of brain–computer interface decoding mistakes: 95% overall accuracy, and 89.66% of incorrect trials detected in evaluations. Also a source-modeling prototype, batch-size scaling in speech decoders, phoneme labels and the Text2P library.

    All five projects
    Error-potential decoding for BCI monitoring

    I implemented trial labeling and adaptive segmentation for missing feedback-onset timestamps, compared event-related potentials on correct and incorrect trials to find informative channels, and designed a four-layer dilated convolutional network with two-stage channel selection and class-weighted loss.

    ECoG source-modeling prototype

    MRI segmentation, CT/MRI registration, finite-element meshing and cortical source spaces; synthetic recordings for testing inverse methods; STTU-Net for cortical signal reconstruction; interactive 3D diagnostics.

    Batch-size scaling in ECoG speech decoders

    Found and characterized how batch size affects accuracy, convergence, stability and generalization in CNN–RNN–CTC speech decoders, using multi-GPU sweeps across powers-of-two batch sizes. Batch-size-informed training reached 15% word error rate, compared with the 25% reference reported in the prior study.

    Context-dependent phoneme labels

    Triphone and word- and sentence-position labels to represent coarticulation and linguistic context, with cross-validated pipelines tracking word, character and phoneme error rates. Confusion analysis and t-SNE/UMAP views identified label schemes that reduced systematic phoneme confusions.

    Text2P

    A modular Python library that uses espeak-ng to produce ARPAbet and IPA phonemes with timing annotations, validated against reference audio.
